432 Hz vs 440 Hz (and 442, 415): what tuner calibration actually changes

By Thomas Willheim · November 18, 2026

A tuner's calibration sets one number: the frequency of A4, the A above middle C. Every other note is worked out from it, so moving A4 from 440 Hz to 432 Hz lowers every note by the same small amount, about a third of a semitone.

That's all calibration does. It doesn't change your tuning, and it doesn't change how the guitar sounds beyond being a little higher or lower. Here's what the common numbers mean and when you should actually touch the setting.

What A4 = 440 Hz means

440 Hz is concert pitch, the reference almost all modern music is tuned to. It was agreed at an international conference in London in 1939, and it's written into the international standard ISO 16 (the current edition is from 1975). Pianos, keyboards, backing tracks, drum machines and nearly every recording you'll play along with assume it.

On a guitar you never play A4 as an open string. Your open A (5th string) is A2, two octaves lower, at 110 Hz: 440 divided by 2, then by 2 again. The other strings follow from the same reference through equal temperament, where each semitone is a factor of the twelfth root of 2.

That's why most tuners default to 440 and why you can leave it there for almost everything.

Why some orchestras tune to 442 or 443

Plenty of orchestras, especially in continental Europe, tune a little higher, usually to 442 or 443 Hz. The usual reason given is that a slightly higher pitch sounds brighter. The orchestra tunes to an A from the oboe, and the oboist matches whatever the orchestra has settled on.

The difference is small. 442 is about 8 cents above 440 (a cent is a hundredth of a semitone), which you won't notice on its own but will hear as a sour, beating sound if you play a 440 guitar next to a 442 piano.

415 Hz and baroque pitch

Ensembles playing baroque music on period instruments often tune to A = 415 Hz. That's very close to a semitone below 440 (an exact semitone below would be 415.3 Hz), which makes life easier for keyboard players: a harpsichord can be shifted down a semitone instead of being retuned from scratch.

415 is a modern convention, not the one true historical pitch. Pitch in the 17th and 18th centuries varied a lot from city to city, and early-music groups use other references too, such as 430 for some classical-era repertoire.

What about 432 Hz?

432 Hz is a reference some musicians prefer, and you'll find a lot of music online labeled as tuned to it. It's often described as more natural, more relaxing, or having healing properties, sometimes with links to mathematics or nature.

Those claims aren't supported by evidence. There's nothing in physics or acoustics that makes 432 special, and no good research showing it does anything for your body or mind that 440 doesn't. In practice it's just a slightly lower reference: 31.8 cents below 440, about a third of a semitone.

If you like how your guitar sounds there, or you want to play along with a track recorded at 432, tuning to it is perfectly fine. Just know that anyone you play with, and any keyboard or backing track, needs to be at 432 too.

Notice that the strings don't all move by the same number of hertz. At 432 the low E drops 1.5 Hz and the high E drops 6 Hz. What stays the same is the ratio: every string moves by the same 31.8 cents. That's how pitch works, and it's why you can't recalibrate by adding or subtracting a fixed number of hertz from each string.

It also means the intervals between strings don't change at all. A guitar tuned at 432 is in tune with itself exactly as well as one tuned at 440; the whole thing has just slid down a bit. String tension drops slightly too, by about 3.6% at 432, which you might feel as a touch more slack on bends.

Common reference pitches, in cents from 440

When you should change calibration

Leave it at 440 unless something you're playing with isn't at 440. The common cases:

  • A piano that isn't at 440. Acoustic pianos drift, usually flat, when they haven't been tuned in a while, and a piano tuner will sometimes leave an older instrument a little below 440 rather than raise it all the way. You can't retune the piano before the gig, so you match it.
  • An orchestra or ensemble at 442, 443 or 415. Ask what they tune to, or take the A they give.
  • A recording that's slightly off. Older records especially can sit between notes, because tape and turntable speeds weren't always exact. If you're learning a song by ear and your open strings sound sour against it, the record may be a few cents away from 440.
  • Music deliberately tuned to 432. Set 432 and you'll be right with it.

Fixed-pitch instruments are what you match: piano, organ, accordion, a backing track. Guitars, basses and singers can move; the piano can't.

How to find the band's reference

You don't need to know the number in advance. Measure it:

  1. Put your tuner in chromatic mode, so it shows whatever note it hears instead of guessing a string.
  2. Have the keyboard player hold an A, ideally the one above middle C (A4) or the one below it. A single sustained note reads more steadily than a chord.
  3. Read how many cents off it is. If it's 20 cents flat, the piano's A4 is about 435 Hz; 8 cents sharp is about 442.
  4. Set the calibration to that number, then play the A again. It should read in tune. Nudge by a hertz if it doesn't.
  5. Tune the guitar as normal. Every string now targets the band's pitch.

For a recording, do the same with a long, clear note from the track, ideally a bass note or a sustained chord root, and check a couple of different notes before you trust the number.

Tip: set it back afterwards. A tuner left at 435 from last week's gig will happily tune you 20 cents flat for the next jam, and everyone else will sound wrong to you.

Calibration isn't a different tuning

If the gap between you and the track is close to a full semitone, you're not looking at a calibration problem. The song is probably in half-step-down tuning (Eb standard), and you should change the tuning, not the reference.

The one overlap is 415: at A4 = 415, standard tuning lands just 1.3 cents below Eb standard at 440. So it works as a half step down in a pinch, but it's cleaner to keep 440 and pick the half-step-down tuning, so your tuner still shows the right note names.
